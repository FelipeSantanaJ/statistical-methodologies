# Wild-cluster bootstrap

## Concept

The wild-cluster bootstrap is a resampling method developed specifically
for inference in regression models with grouped data when the number of
clusters is small — the setting in which the cluster-robust variance
estimator (CRVE) loses its asymptotic guarantees and systematically
understates the real uncertainty.

The core idea rests on two design choices:

1. **Resample residuals, not observations or entire clusters.** A
   conventional cluster bootstrap draws entire clusters with replacement —
   which, with few clusters, produces replicates with coarse repetition
   (for example, one cluster appearing three times and another not at all)
   and an effectively too-small sample space to form a good reference
   distribution.
2. **Perturb by a random sign, preserving the within-cluster dependence
   structure.** Instead of resampling, the method keeps every original
   observation and multiplies each cluster's residuals by a single random
   value drawn for that cluster (typically +1 or −1, the "Rademacher
   distribution") — preserving exactly the correlation pattern within each
   cluster, because every observation in that cluster is perturbed as a
   block, in the same direction.

## Mathematical formulation

Consider the linear model with $G$ clusters:

$$
Y_{ig} = X_{ig}'\beta + \varepsilon_{ig}, \quad g = 1, \dots, G
$$

The interest is testing $H_0: \beta_j = \beta_j^0$ (often $\beta_j^0 = 0$)
for a specific coefficient $\beta_j$.

**Step 1 — restricted fit.** Estimate the model imposing $H_0$ (the
"restricted" model), obtaining residuals $\tilde u_{ig}$ and fitted values
$\tilde Y_{ig}$ under the restriction.

**Step 2 — sign perturbation.** For each replicate $b = 1, \dots, B$, draw a
sign $v_g^{(b)} \in \{-1, +1\}$ **per cluster** (not per observation),
usually with probability 1/2 for each value:

$$
v_g^{(b)} \sim \text{Rademacher}(0.5)
$$

**Step 3 — synthetic data.** Build a synthetic dependent variable:

$$
Y_{ig}^{*(b)} = \tilde Y_{ig} + v_g^{(b)} \, \tilde u_{ig}
$$

Note that $v_g^{(b)}$ does not vary within cluster $g$ — every observation
in that cluster gets the same sign in that replicate, preserving the
within-cluster correlation.

**Step 4 — refit and statistic.** Refit the (unrestricted) model to the
synthetic data $Y^{*(b)}$, obtaining $\hat\beta_j^{*(b)}$ and its
cluster-robust standard error $\widehat{SE}^{*(b)}(\hat\beta_j)$, forming
the bootstrap statistic:

$$
t^{*(b)} = \frac{\hat\beta_j^{*(b)} - \beta_j^0}{\widehat{SE}^{*(b)}(\hat\beta_j)}
$$

**Step 5 — p-value.** Repeating steps 2 through 4 for $B$ replicates
(typically $B = 999$), the bootstrap p-value is:

$$
p^{*} = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^{B} \mathbb{1}\left[ \, |t^{*(b)}| \geq |t_{\text{obs}}| \, \right]
$$

with $t_{\text{obs}}$ the t-statistic computed on the real data (under the
unrestricted model), using the conventional cluster-robust standard error.

With $G$ clusters, there are at most $2^G$ distinct sign combinations — with
$G=6$, for example, 64 combinations, which still leaves a reasonably
fine-grained p-value even when enumerating exhaustively instead of
sampling.

## Assumptions

- **Model correctly specified under $H_0$.** The restricted fit, used to
  generate the perturbed residuals, needs to reasonably reflect the real
  relationship between the variables — a misspecified model produces
  residuals that do not represent the true error structure well.
- **Independence across clusters.** As with the CRVE, the method's validity
  depends on clusters being independent of one another — residual
  dependence between clusters is not fixed by the wild-cluster bootstrap.
- **Symmetry of the sign distribution.** The standard choice of Rademacher
  signs (+1/−1 with equal probability) works well in most cases;
  alternative sign distributions exist in the literature, but Rademacher is
  the most widely used for its robustness and simplicity.
- **Minimum number of clusters.** With extremely few clusters (2 or 3),
  even the space of $2^G$ possible signs becomes too small to form a useful
  reference distribution — the method remains superior to the asymptotic
  CRVE at that extreme, but with weaker guarantees.

## Hypotheses

- $H_0$: $\beta_j = \beta_j^0$ (commonly $\beta_j^0 = 0$, "the coefficient
  is zero").
- $H_1$: $\beta_j \neq \beta_j^0$.
- Test statistic: $t = (\hat\beta_j - \beta_j^0) / \widehat{SE}_{\text{cluster}}(\hat\beta_j)$,
  computed on the real data and compared to the empirical distribution of
  $t^{*(b)}$ generated by the bootstrap, rather than to a theoretical t
  distribution.
- Confidence interval: can be obtained by inverting the test (searching for
  the range of $\beta_j^0$ values not rejected by the bootstrap test), a
  more computationally demanding approach, but more accurate than
  approximating the interval from the raw percentiles of the
  $\hat\beta_j^{*(b)}$ distribution.

## Interpretation

A low wild-cluster bootstrap p-value is evidence that the observed
statistic would be unusual even under simulated variation consistent with
the null hypothesis and the data's actual dependence structure — a more
trustworthy reading than the asymptotic CRVE precisely in the few-cluster
range where the latter tends to fail.

As with any inference-correction method, the wild-cluster bootstrap does
not solve the original design's causal-identification problems (omitted
variables, non-random selection) — it only produces a more faithful
reference distribution for testing hypotheses about the estimated
coefficient, given that the model and design are already valid for other
reasons.

## Limitations

- **Still depends on a minimum number of useful clusters.** With very small
  $G$ (2 or 3), the space of $2^G$ possible signs is too small to form a
  good distribution — at that extreme, randomization inference, when
  applicable to the design, tends to be preferable.
- **Sensitivity to the restricted model's specification.** Residuals from a
  poorly fitted model under $H_0$ propagate that specification error
  throughout the entire bootstrap distribution.
- **Computational cost.** Each replicate requires refitting the model —
  with $B$ in the thousands and complex models, the total cost can be
  significant, though it is typically manageable at usual applied-work
  scale.

### Comparison with other methods for inference with few clusters

Wild-cluster bootstrap, cluster-robust standard errors, and randomization
inference all answer the same problem: reliable inference when observations
within a cluster are not independent and few clusters are available.

- **Cluster-robust standard errors (CRVE)** are the standard, simple, fast
  choice, but lose reliability precisely when there are few clusters — the
  setting where wild-cluster bootstrap and randomization inference become
  relevant.
- **Randomization inference** is preferable when the experiment's own
  assignment mechanism is known and simple enough to enumerate (or sample)
  the possible reassignments directly — it requires fitting no regression
  model at all, only recomputing a statistic under treatment
  reassignments. It works best when the effect of interest comes from a
  direct comparison between groups, with no additional controls.
- **Wild-cluster bootstrap** is the more natural choice when the effect of
  interest comes from a regression model with controls or a richer
  structure (fixed effects, covariates), where there is no simple
  assignment mechanism to reassign directly — the bootstrap perturbs the
  already-fitted model's residuals, instead of reassigning treatment to
  units.

When both methods are applicable to the same problem, running both and
comparing conclusions is a valuable robustness check: agreement between
randomization inference and wild-cluster bootstrap reinforces confidence in
the result; disagreement is a signal that it is worth investigating further
before reporting a conclusion.

## Example

A clinic chain tests a new scheduling protocol across 8 locations (4
treatment, 4 control), measuring average patient wait time, with additional
controls in the model (day of week, time slot). The estimated treatment
coefficient is −6.5 minutes, with a conventional cluster-robust standard
error of 2.1, giving t ≈ −3.1 and an asymptotic p-value of roughly 0.003
(apparently highly significant).

Running the wild-cluster bootstrap with $B = 999$ replicates over the 8
clusters (drawing Rademacher signs per clinic in each replicate, refitting
the restricted model, and recomputing the t-statistic each time), the
observed statistic of −3.1 falls at a percentile corresponding to a
bootstrap p-value of roughly 0.04 — still significant at 5%, but with a
substantially tighter margin than the asymptotic p-value of 0.003
suggested.

![Wild-cluster bootstrap distribution from 999 replicates compared to the asymptotic t reference, for a design with few clusters](../../../assets/figures/wild-cluster-bootstrap-distribution-en.png)

The gap between the two p-values (0.003 vs. 0.04) illustrates why the
asymptotic CRVE tends to be excessively optimistic with few clusters — the
qualitative conclusion (a significant effect) still holds in this example,
but the real confidence margin is much narrower than the first reading
suggested, and a slightly weaker result could easily stop being significant
under the wild-cluster correction.
